Chapter 01 / Childhood, Indonesia

The Boy Who Wanted to Know

Prologue — A Question I Used to Fear

For most of my life, I have carried one question with me: Who will I become?

As a teenager, it rarely felt like an exciting question. There were too many things I wanted to try and too many ways my future could go wrong. I worried that I might grow older without discovering what I was especially good at, or without doing anything meaningful with the possibilities in front of me.

For a long time, my answer was to do more. Learn more, take on more, become more capable. I thought that if I kept moving, eventually I would arrive at the person I was supposed to be.

It took me years to notice that becoming someone and getting to know myself were not quite the same thing.

But that part comes later. The story begins with a boy who wanted to know how everything worked.

A Workshop Full of Questions

My father had a workshop in Indonesia. As a child, I thought it was an extraordinary place. There were screwdrivers, pliers, machines, and things I could take apart long before I understood what they were made for.

I liked working with my hands, particularly on my bicycle. If the chain came loose, I wanted to understand how to put it back. If a brake stopped working, I was interested in what happened between pulling the lever and slowing the wheel. Sometimes something needed fixing. Other times I just wanted to see what was inside.

My father was curious in much the same way. He showed me how to use tools, taught me basic soldering, and introduced me to small electronics kits. I remember learning to read resistor colour bands in elementary school. I didn't yet understand the circuits in any meaningful depth, but it amazed me that a few coloured stripes could represent a value.

When computers entered my life, they became another thing to investigate. I tried basic coding, explored software, and even opened up laptops to understand the hardware. I wasn't always sure what I was doing. Curiosity was usually several steps ahead of my knowledge.

And that was true beyond the workshop, too.

The Trouble With Curiosity

I was an energetic kid. I loved spending time outside, being around friends, and exploring whatever happened to catch my interest. But I was also impulsive. I cared about ideas like fairness and justice, yet I didn't always know how to act on them appropriately.

Sometimes I understood that something was probably wrong and still wanted to find out what would happen. I'd get into trouble at school, tell myself I was going to behave differently, and end up in trouble again. I remember reaching a point when I was genuinely tired of that cycle. I wanted to be better, but wanting it and knowing how to stop myself weren't the same thing.

One particular mistake has stayed with me.

My father had taught me some basic driving under supervision. When I was in sixth grade, my parents went away on a trip. I was explicitly told not to drive without an adult. I did it anyway, and I hit a parked vehicle.

Nobody was injured, thankfully. The outcome could have been far worse, and what I did was dangerous. I knew I'd crossed a serious line. Before my parents returned, I spent days imagining how angry my father would be.

When they came home, something unexpected happened. My father greeted us warmly and brought out the souvenirs he'd bought on the trip. Only after everyone had settled in did he ask me to sit down and explain what had happened.

He listened. He asked about the sequence of events. Then he took me to see the damage and face the situation.

I had expected shouting. Instead, I was given space to tell the truth about a mistake I already felt terrible about.

I don't think the lesson was that mistakes don't matter. They do. Mine had put other people at risk. What stayed with me was the way my father approached the person who had made it.

Two Ways of Seeing the World

My father wasn't always patient or easy to agree with. There were things he did that I questioned then and still see differently now. Neither of my parents was perfect; they were learning how to be parents, too.

But as I grew older, I began recognising the thought behind many of my father's decisions. He liked telling stories about situations he'd faced and the choices he'd made. Without announcing that he was giving me a lesson, he kept nudging me to look at consequences and consider the other sides of a problem.

Years later, he explained why he rarely became angry when I brought home a disappointing grade. A child coming home with a bad result, he said, might already be feeling awful. Why make that child feel even worse?

My mother taught me something different. She was warm and friendly with people, including strangers and people doing ordinary jobs. I noticed how an interaction could change when she treated the person behind the counter with genuine ease and kindness. Things often became less complicated because the people involved felt comfortable talking to one another.

I didn't grow up thinking of these as lessons in logic and empathy. I was busy taking things apart, playing with friends, and making the kind of mistakes children make.

Only much later did I begin to see those two influences together: an invitation to think carefully, and a reminder not to forget the person in front of me.

As a child, I didn't have the words for any of that. I was still trying to understand the world outside me. It would be a while before I started asking equally difficult questions about myself.

Chapter 02 / High School, Indonesia

Learning to Belong

A New School, No Familiar Faces

For most of elementary and middle school, I was surrounded by people I had known for years. Friendship was part of the background of everyday life. I didn't have to think very hard about how to begin one.

Then I changed schools for high school. Suddenly, almost everybody was a stranger.

I loved being around people, but I wasn't prepared for the uncomfortable beginning of a friendship, when someone has no reason yet to know who you are. I had to learn how to introduce myself, join a conversation, and find my place among groups that already had their own dynamics.

At first, I wanted to fit in. But I also didn't want to change everything about myself just to be accepted.

I had opinions, and sometimes I could be very idealistic about them. I gradually learned that being true to myself didn't require asking someone I'd just met to understand every part of me immediately. People needed time to get to know one another. I could keep my principles and still be approachable; I could listen without pretending that I agreed with everything.

That balance took practice.

Seeing People as People

The more people I met, the more I found myself noticing the way they interacted.

I liked watching social dynamics. How did two people who were both easy to talk to end up arguing? Why did someone act differently in one group than in another? How much of an interaction was shaped by emotion, by expectations, or by something nobody had said directly?

I didn't have a theory for all of it. I was simply interested.

I also noticed how easily schools divided people into groups. A group developed a reputation, and before long someone could be judged by who they spent time with. I could see those boundaries, but they rarely felt important enough to stop me from getting to know someone.

Years later, a friend told me I had been the kind of person who moved between different friendship groups and sometimes brought people together who otherwise wouldn't have met. That surprised me. I hadn't set out to be anyone's bridge. From my perspective, I just liked people in different groups and wanted to know them as individuals.

High school was also where I discovered how much I enjoyed organizing. Working with a team, dividing responsibilities, and seeing people with different personalities contribute to something together was exciting to me. I began noticing that others sometimes counted on me, and I liked being someone they could depend on.

Without realizing it, I was starting to associate being useful with being valuable. At the time, it mostly felt good.

The Future Was Getting Closer

My parents had never treated grades as a measure of how much they cared about me. They were pleased when I did well, but a bad result wasn't followed by anger. Eventually, I came to understand that studying was something I had to take responsibility for myself.

That was liberating. I wasn't trying to earn my parents' approval every time I opened a textbook.

But taking responsibility for my education meant I had to think about where it was leading.

I liked technical things. I liked people. I liked working with my hands and being part of organizations. I believed that, with enough effort, I could learn almost anything I chose to learn. I just didn't know which direction I wanted to choose.

Sometimes I worried that having so many interests meant I would never become especially good at anything. I wanted a future I could commit myself to, something I could pour my energy into and call meaningful. But I couldn't picture what that was yet.

The question that had once been somewhere far away began to feel urgent.

Who will I become?

I didn't know. And as graduation approached, I started feeling that not knowing was a problem I needed to fix.

I was about to make a decision that would move me much farther from everything familiar than changing schools ever had.

Chapter 03 / 2019, Indonesia → Germany

The First Goodbye

A Decision I Wasn't Ready For

When I finished high school in 2019, I still wasn't sure what I wanted to study. I considered different subjects, including medicine, and I wasn't even completely certain whether I'd go to university in Indonesia or abroad.

Eventually, I chose Germany.

It sounds much more carefully planned when I say it in one sentence. In reality, I was far less prepared than I should have been.

I began learning German around May, after finishing high school. My private lessons took place roughly twice a week for two hours at a time. Other people were preparing for years; I'd only just started.

I took my B1 examination in July. I failed. In October, I took it again and passed.

I was relieved to have the certificate, but I didn't yet understand the difference between passing an exam and being able to live in a language. I would learn that difference quickly enough.

At home, I'd grown up with many things being handled for me. Someone helped with daily chores. My mother often helped me figure out practical arrangements. I wasn't used to researching every step or managing all the ordinary details of living independently.

I wanted a new life, but I hadn't learned very much about taking care of one.

In November 2019, I left Indonesia anyway.

At the Airport

I remember my parents crying when we said goodbye.

I'd seen them emotional before, but I hadn't seen them cry like that because I was leaving. Until then, Germany had mostly been a plan: a language certificate, a flight, a course, and hopefully a university education after that.

Suddenly it was also a separation from the people who had been present through almost every day of my life.

I'd always known I loved my parents. At the airport, I felt what it might actually mean to miss them.

I landed in Berlin and continued to Kiel. A friend met me, so the first part didn't feel quite as lonely as it could have. I had help finding my feet, somewhere to stay temporarily, and a room of my own a couple of days later.

For a little while, I was still excited. I had arrived. Everything was unfamiliar, but that was part of the point.

Then I moved into my room.

I remember putting down my things, sitting at my desk, and suddenly crying.

There hadn't been one terrible event. I hadn't received bad news. I was simply alone, and for the first time it felt as though everything that happened next was in my hands.

At home, there had always been someone to ask or something familiar to fall back on. Here, even simple things required decisions I didn't yet know how to make. I couldn't casually talk to people in the language around me. I couldn't assume somebody else would arrange the next step.

I had wanted freedom. Sitting at that desk, I began to understand that freedom came with responsibility I hadn't practiced carrying.

I don't think I told many people about how I felt that day. I wasn't even sure how to explain it to myself.

I eventually got up, carried on unpacking, and followed the plan I'd made for my first months in Germany. That plan was going to be tested almost immediately.

Chapter 04 / 2019–2022, Kiel

Learning to Stand Alone

A Classroom I Couldn't Follow

My plan was to take a B2 German course in Kiel, then move elsewhere for a preparatory course before Studienkolleg.

On paper, the sequence made sense. In reality, I could barely follow my first class.

Some days I couldn't understand the instructions. Even when I knew I was lost, I didn't know how to ask for help in German. The language I had passed an examination in seemed completely different from the language people were speaking around me.

Eventually, my teacher approached me and suggested moving down to a lower level. She wasn't cruel about it. She explained that I could stay, but she had other students to teach and couldn't stop the entire class to catch me up whenever I struggled.

I understood her position. That didn't make it easier to hear.

If I couldn't keep up in a language course, how was I supposed to survive a German university? I had crossed the world with so little preparation. I was afraid I'd have to go home having failed before I had really begun.

I was also afraid to tell my parents how lost I felt. They had watched me leave and believed I had a plan. I couldn't bear the thought of explaining that I might not be able to follow it.

One evening, with no real idea of what to do, I prayed.

I'd prayed before, but I don't remember ever speaking to God that way. I wasn't repeating familiar lines or asking for a convenient result. I admitted that I was frightened, that I didn't know how to handle things, and that I couldn't carry everything by myself. I cried while I spoke.

Afterward I felt unexpectedly lighter. Nothing practical had changed. I still didn't understand the course, and the decisions were still mine to make. But acknowledging that I needed help changed how I could face the next morning.

That experience became an important beginning in my relationship with faith.

The Work That Came Next

I started going to the library after class. I reviewed the pages we'd studied, translated words I didn't understand, worked through the exercises, and read ahead so that the next lesson wouldn't be entirely unfamiliar.

It was slow, repetitive work. I often couldn't tell whether I was improving. But I had something concrete I could do, one page at a time.

Eventually I decided not to move to another city for the preparatory course I'd originally planned. I told my mother that I wanted to stay in Kiel and continue learning German. She allowed me to make that choice and supported me, even though it wasn't the plan I'd told her before leaving.

I built a routine around studying on my own, often getting up at seven and working through textbooks until around noon.

Then the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted everything. The German course I had planned to take was canceled, and daily life became much smaller.

It wasn't a welcome situation. But it gave me something I hadn't allowed myself much of before moving to Germany: time to prepare properly.

So I kept studying. Some days I felt productive. Other days I was just returning to the same words and rules, trying again.

A Conversation in the Kitchen

Around the spring of 2020, I was in the shared kitchen when someone started talking to me in German.

At first I didn't think much of it. Then I realized I knew what they were saying.

And I could answer.

It wasn't a remarkable conversation to anybody listening. My German was still hesitant, and I certainly didn't understand everything. But for the first time I found myself participating instead of standing outside the language, trying to decode it.

I remember being almost shocked by that realization.

Months earlier, I'd been unable to ask my teacher for help. Now someone could speak to me, and I could speak back.

There was no dramatic certificate or announcement. Just a conversation in a kitchen that showed me I'd made progress I hadn't known how to measure.

I still had a long way to go.

When I entered Studienkolleg in Kiel in 2022, I struggled with language and coursework. Sometimes I understood the teacher; sometimes I had to reconstruct the lesson afterward. But I'd seen what could happen when I kept trying through uncertainty.

At the end of 2022, I finished Studienkolleg. In 2023, I began university.

I had made it through a period when I genuinely wasn't sure I could stay in Germany. I learned to trust that I could adapt, even if the beginning was uncomfortable.

That confidence would become one of my strengths. Eventually, though, I would start confusing the ability to push through a hard situation with the idea that I should push through everything.

Chapter 05 / 2023–2025

The Race to Become Someone

Everything, All at Once

University brought a different kind of challenge. My German was better than when I'd first arrived, but understanding a conversation and following a technical lecture were still very different things.

Sometimes I came home and worked through the material twice: once to understand what had been said, and once to understand the subject itself. Laboratory work was particularly intimidating because I needed to communicate with classmates while also trying to understand an unfamiliar experiment.

I learned and adapted, as I had before. But I also became increasingly determined to move forward as quickly as possible.

I wanted to finish university. I wanted to build professional experience. I wanted to be there for friends, participate in projects, and build a future with the person I was in a relationship with. I wanted to become the kind of adult who had things under control.

In one semester, I took eleven examinations while also helping with the Indonesian stand at Kieler Woche. The GSM job came later, but the instinct was already the same: if something mattered, I'd find a way to fit it in.

Sometimes I genuinely enjoyed that pace. I liked discovering that I could take on unfamiliar work and figure it out. I liked having people around me and seeing projects move forward.

When friends asked how I managed everything, I thought the answer was obvious. Organize your time. Do the work. Rest when you're tired.

I didn't understand how limited that answer was.

Becoming the Dependable One

I also liked helping people.

From school onward, I'd been interested in how people think and why they make the decisions they do. As I grew more confident in my reasoning, I became quicker to see possible solutions when someone brought me a problem.

Sometimes I was genuinely helpful. Other times I offered my answer too forcefully, convinced that if somebody could just see the problem the way I saw it, things would improve.

I cared about my friends, but I wasn't always giving them space to make their own choices.

And there was another reason I found it difficult to step back: being dependable had become part of how I understood my value.

I liked knowing someone could turn to me. I liked having something useful to contribute. If a problem needed solving, I wanted to be the person who could solve it.

During those years I was also in a serious relationship. We were together for about three years, and I began arranging much of my imagined future around the life I hoped we would build together.

There was always a next step to prepare for, another responsibility to accept, another way I thought I could become more capable.

I was in a hurry.

The Limits I Didn't Want to See

For a while, everything appeared to be moving forward.

Assignments were being completed. People trusted me. Projects continued. I could point to things I'd done and responsibilities I'd taken on.

Because I was still functioning, I assumed that meant I was fine.

But slowly, I was becoming exhausted. I had divided my attention among university, people, work, and the future I was trying to build. Sometimes nothing seemed to get the best of me, even though I was spending nearly all of myself.

I wasn't paying attention to capacity. I thought tiredness was simply a problem I could solve with better scheduling and more determination.

Even when I began experiencing burnout, I kept going. Stopping felt like falling behind, and falling behind frightened me.

The strange thing is that I wasn't always unhappy in any obvious way. There were good days, good people, and experiences I valued. I just wasn't asking whether the overall direction of my life felt right for me.

I could tell you what I needed to achieve next. I could tell you what someone else needed help with. I could explain the kind of future I was working toward.

But if you asked whether I was happy living that life, I wouldn't have known how to answer.

I had spent so much time trying to become someone that I had stopped getting to know the person doing all the work.

Eventually, the future I'd been rushing toward changed in a way I couldn't fix by making another schedule.

Chapter 06 / October 2025

Choosing to Begin Again

When the Future Changed

In October 2025, my relationship of three years ended.

I don't want to turn a relationship involving another person into a story that belongs only to me. There were two people in it, and there are parts I don't think I need to tell publicly. What I can describe is what happened inside me afterward.

I'd imagined a future with someone I loved. I'd thought about my studies, my career, and the kind of life we might build together. When the relationship ended, the future around which I'd arranged so much of my life suddenly looked different.

I was hurt. But I was also forced to stop moving long enough to notice how I'd been living.

I began questioning things I'd pushed aside for years. Why did I feel the need to become successful so quickly? Why was I so uncomfortable being seen as weak? Why did it matter so much that people thought of me as someone who could do everything?

Slowly, I began recognising how much of my self-worth had depended on other people finding me capable, useful, and dependable.

When I was with friends or busy helping someone, I could feel secure in that role. When I was alone, the old insecurities returned. I worried about whether I was enough, whether I'd do anything meaningful, and whether all the potential I imagined I had would ever turn into something real.

It was uncomfortable to acknowledge that. I'd spent so long trying to be someone other people could rely on that I'd become reluctant to show when I needed anyone myself.

I had been learning how to build a future. I hadn't learned how to care for the person who would have to live in it.

Learning to Love Myself

I decided to step back from the usual pace of university for roughly a year and give myself space to ask different questions.

I didn't want to use the time to construct a new, more impressive version of Alvin as quickly as possible. I wanted to figure out what mattered to me when I wasn't trying to meet a deadline or someone else's expectation.

I began with an idea that was simple to say and surprisingly difficult to practice:

Accept what I cannot change, and take responsibility for improving what I can.

There were things about myself I'd spent too much energy wishing were different: my height, my background, aspects of my appearance. I wanted to stop treating those as failures that needed correcting.

But there were also habits, beliefs, and ways of treating people that I could change. I didn't want self-acceptance to mean refusing to grow. I wanted growth to come from caring about myself, not from disliking who I already was.

One of my first experiments was with vulnerability.

For years I'd wanted to be perceived as strong, the person who had an answer and rarely needed help. So I began practicing the opposite of what came naturally: saying when I didn't understand, admitting when I was struggling, and letting people I trusted see parts of me I usually kept hidden.

I also looked more closely at my emotions.

When anxiety or insecurity appeared, my usual response had been to push it away or get busy enough that I didn't have to think about it. I thought ignoring a feeling would make it less powerful. Instead, I often found it waiting for me later.

I started trying to acknowledge what I was feeling before deciding what, if anything, to do about it. Sometimes that helped. Sometimes the feeling stayed. I was learning that recognising an emotion didn't mean allowing it to make every decision for me.

A Different Relationship With Problems

Around that time I read Mark Manson's *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck*.

The book pushed me to think about what deserved my attention, which problems I was choosing to carry, and why I had spent so long imagining that happiness would arrive once I'd finally solved everything.

I remember crying near the end. It wasn't that the book gave me a complete answer. It helped me consider that a meaningful life would probably always contain difficult things, and that I could learn to choose and approach those difficulties differently.

I started thinking about capacity and boundaries. About caring for someone without needing to solve their life. About being responsible without turning responsibility into a reason to exhaust myself.

Those ideas were not instantly easy to live by. Sometimes I understood them clearly and still behaved the old way the next day.

But the direction had changed.

I wasn't asking how quickly I could reach the next milestone. I was beginning to ask what kind of relationship I wanted with the life I was already living.

Chapter 07 / 2026 →

Still Becoming

Learning to See Myself More Clearly

I wish I could say that once I decided to change, everything started making sense. It didn't.

I would notice an old habit, understand why I wanted to stop, and then catch myself doing the same thing again. Some days I felt I'd made real progress. Other days I wondered whether I was just becoming better at explaining my problems without actually changing them.

Gradually, though, I began noticing small differences in how I responded to ordinary situations.

For a long time, when someone I cared about had a problem, I felt an urge to solve it. I might spend hours thinking through the situation, explaining the options, and trying to prevent a decision I believed would end badly.

That usually came from genuine concern. But my concern could also become a way of taking responsibility for a life that wasn't mine.

I began separating two things I'd once treated as almost the same: understanding another person's feelings and carrying responsibility for them.

I could listen to a friend without immediately giving advice. I could offer my perspective and let them decide differently. I could stay present without making their problem the centre of my own day.

Learning boundaries didn't make me want to care less. It helped me understand how to care while remembering that I had limits, too.

Thinking, Feeling, and Being Wrong

I'd always been proud of my ability to examine problems from more than one perspective. It was something I'd learned from watching my father think through decisions, and it had become useful in studying, working, and helping people.

But a carefully reasoned answer can still be incomplete.

Sometimes I didn't have enough information. Sometimes my own insecurities were shaping the questions I asked. Sometimes somebody needed me to understand how they felt, not explain the most logical solution.

I started paying more attention to the relationship between logic, emotion, and responsibility. When did a situation actually require me to act? When was I reacting because I couldn't tolerate uncertainty? Was I helping another person make their own decision, or trying to make it for them?

I also became more comfortable admitting that an opinion I held might be wrong.

I still have strong beliefs, and I want to be thoughtful about them. But I don't want my identity to depend on protecting every conclusion I've reached. What I believe today is the best understanding I have with the information available to me today. If I encounter something better tomorrow, I want to be willing to change my mind.

That doesn't make the person I was yesterday worthless. It means I'm learning.

Practicing With Other People

Some of these lessons became very real during my time leading Organizational Affairs at PPI Jerman.

I was working with volunteers who had different commitments, working styles, and strengths. I couldn't assume everyone would plan, communicate, or make decisions the way I did. Sometimes someone needed direction. Sometimes they needed room to find an answer themselves.

I wanted people to feel comfortable trying something unfamiliar, making mistakes, and asking for help before a problem became bigger. That required me to stay composed when things didn't go to plan and to be willing to admit when my own judgment was incomplete.

I also had to learn to accept help instead of treating every unresolved issue as proof that I hadn't done enough.

One memory from the leadership camp stays with me. After the programme had finished, the kitchen team was still cleaning while other divisions had completed their work. I asked people from those divisions to come and help. They did.

It wasn't an impressive achievement on paper. It was simply a moment when people noticed that their teammates still needed them.

I recognized a version of something my mother had shown me years earlier: caring becomes meaningful in the way we treat people, especially when nobody is keeping score.

And I recognised something else. Being responsible for a team didn't mean doing everything myself. Sometimes my job was to make it easier for people to help one another.

Finding Curiosity Again

Outside of organizations and university, I began paying attention to my own routines, health, and the way I spent my time. Some changes were physical. Others were as small as learning to admit I was tired or letting myself enjoy a day without needing to turn it into an accomplishment.

I also began building things again.

ORZEN and AI Agents Studio became spaces where I could follow an idea, experiment with AI-assisted development, see what actually worked, and change course when it didn't. They weren't finished answers. They were projects I could learn through.

Sometimes I look at them and recognize the same curiosity I had as a child in my father's workshop.

Back then I wanted to understand how a bicycle brake worked or what happened inside a laptop. Now I ask questions about software, planning, decision-making, and how people interact with systems.

The objects have changed. The instinct to explore hasn't.

I also notice that I'm beginning to ask those questions about myself with more patience.

What kind of work brings out my curiosity? When am I helping because I care, and when am I helping because I need to feel needed? What do I really value? What should I keep, and what should I be willing to reconsider?

I don't always know. But not knowing doesn't frighten me in quite the same way anymore.

Epilogue — An Unfinished Story

I'm still ambitious. I still like achieving difficult things, being there for people, and working on ideas that matter to me.

I also still get things wrong. There are old habits I haven't completely unlearned, situations I don't know how to handle, and parts of myself I'm only beginning to understand.

For a long time, I looked at all of that uncertainty as evidence that I was falling behind. I wanted to become a finished version of myself, somebody who finally had every answer and could stop worrying about whether he was enough.

Now, when I think about the future, I'm curious about the people I'll meet, the problems I'll encounter, the things I'll build, and the ideas I may eventually change my mind about.

I want to see what all of that teaches me. I want to live through the process instead of treating it as an obstacle between me and the person I'm supposed to become.

The question I've carried since childhood is still here.

Who will I become?

I don't know yet.

And for the first time, I'm genuinely excited to find out.

I'm still becoming. And for once, I'm not in a hurry.
