Chapter 01

Starting Without a Map

A friend from university told me that GSM was looking for a working student and recommended me. I applied without a carefully defined career plan. I wanted to challenge myself, experience a professional workplace in Germany, and learn what I was like when the work was no longer a university assignment.

My first weeks were overwhelming. There were funding rules, different programmes, unfamiliar software, and procedures that seemed to connect to one another in ways I couldn't yet see. And almost all of the explanations happened in German. Understanding a conversation was one thing; understanding a whole operational process through it was another.

I wrote everything down. After training, I went over my notes at home and tried to walk through each task in my head until the sequence made sense. After a few weeks, I was trusted to handle some projects myself. My manager commented on how quickly I was learning, but I remember the work it took to reach that point more clearly than the compliment.

Chapter 02

The Story Behind an Invoice

GSM provides coaching and programmes that help people prepare for employment or return to working life in Germany. Some participants are getting used to a new country; others may have spent years away from employment. Programmes can be funded by institutions such as the Jobcenter or Agentur für Arbeit.

The people attending a class are not simply names on a spreadsheet. Their participation has to be approved, their coaching hours have limits, and what actually happens during a session has to be documented accurately before the organisation can bill for it.

Every month I compared digital attendance records with physical documents signed by participants and coaches. I checked the approved funding or voucher, the number of coaching hours still available, and the records relating to absences. When the digital and signed records didn't agree, I contacted coaches to clarify what had happened.

Only after the supporting information was in order could I calculate the billable hours, prepare and send the invoice to the responsible funding institution, and record the relevant bookkeeping entries in Agenda. Sometimes a funding office contacted me directly about a question or a correction, and I followed up on it.

It was detailed work, but it made sense to me: the number on the final invoice had to be supported by the history behind it.

Chapter 03

Four or Five Locations, Many Different Cases

Depending on how assignments were divided, I recall being responsible for around four or five locations and approximately sixty coaching projects or classes in a typical month. Those are estimates from memory, not audited counts. A project wasn't necessarily one invoice or one participant; each could have its own changing attendance records, people joining or finishing, and unresolved questions.

I needed a way to tell what had already been checked, what was waiting for a coach, and what could move to invoicing. That meant making the early validation work a priority. If I needed somebody else to correct an attendance record, asking early gave them time to respond.

When a monthly accounting period required a provisional entry before every supporting detail was complete, I kept it identifiable and followed through with corrections in the subsequent period. The aim wasn't to ignore a discrepancy but to keep the record traceable while the underlying information was resolved.

Over time, I was working independently with my assigned locations and projects. Independence didn't mean knowing everything. It meant knowing what I could verify myself, what needed clarification, and how to keep track until the case was actually settled.

Chapter 04

A Number That Looked Right

One case taught me why a spreadsheet total shouldn't be accepted simply because it looks ordinary.

According to the existing calculation, a participant had already used all of their approved coaching hours. But based on the programme timeline, the participant should still have been eligible for more. I couldn't see an obvious error in the current month's figures.

So I went back through the older entries and recalculated the hours step by step. Eventually I found a calculation mistake that had been made before I took over the project. It wasn't visible at a glance; the incorrect balance had simply been carried forward.

What stayed with me was the difference between checking a result and checking how that result had been produced. Sometimes the useful question isn't whether today's number adds up. It's when the underlying numbers first stopped agreeing.

Chapter 05

Making the Repetitive Work Easier to Review

I enjoyed learning the existing processes, but I also began seeing where repetitive manual comparisons could be handled more systematically.

One group of short coaching programmes had participants changing almost every month. Some classes involved as many as roughly twenty people. There wasn't a suitable Excel template for those particular projects, so comparing participant names and programme status with the previous month had largely been manual work.

I made my own Excel template to match names across months and help identify which cases were ready to bill and which were still continuing. I also developed personal tracking and plausibility checks for other recurring work: project status, mismatched balances, and participants nearing the end of a permitted programme period.

I improved fields in an Excel–Word invoice template that already existed before I joined. I didn't build the original integration from scratch, and these tools weren't rolled out as company-wide systems. They were practical aids for my own workload.

What I liked about the process was deciding which comparisons a spreadsheet could do consistently and where human judgment was still necessary. An automatic flag was useful because it told me what to investigate, not because it could decide the truth on its own.

Chapter 06

The People Behind the Paperwork

Although my work revolved around documents, it depended on conversations. Coaches helped clarify records I couldn't resolve at my desk. Colleagues were patient with questions, even when I felt I had asked the same thing several times. My manager supported my university schedule.

Some evenings I started work late because I'd been at university all day. I remember quieter conversations with my manager in the office during those hours. They made the workplace feel less intimidating than it had in my first weeks.

I joined GSM hoping to find out how I'd manage in a German professional environment. By the end, I had learned to handle operational responsibility, examine inconsistencies, communicate with the people who held missing information, and improve small parts of a process without losing sight of why the process existed.

I didn't become someone who had every answer. I became more comfortable following the evidence until I could explain the answer I had.
