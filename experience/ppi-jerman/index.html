Chapter 01

Why I Chose to Lead

I had enjoyed organizing things since high school, but for a while I was skeptical about the student-association world. From the outside, some of it looked more political than purposeful.

That changed when I became involved with PPI Kiel. Working with a chairperson who had a clear reason for what they wanted to do showed me how much an organization could mean to the people around it. I served as secretary and eventually became chairperson of the Kiel branch myself.

Attending LDK 2.0, a national student leadership camp, changed something else. I met leaders whose titles had once sounded intimidating and discovered that they were ordinary people who were still learning. I didn't need to have every answer before accepting responsibility.

Kiel was a relatively small branch. There had been moments when the national organization felt distant, as though a question from a smaller city might not be noticed. I initially considered joining PPI Jerman's Organizational Affairs Department as an ordinary member, but people who knew my work encouraged me to apply to lead it.

I remembered the experience of having a question and not knowing whom to ask. I wanted the next branch leader to feel that somebody would listen.

Chapter 02

A Department That Connects People

Organizational Affairs isn't simply about internal documents. Much of its work involves maintaining the relationship between PPI Jerman and local branches across Germany.

A newly elected committee may need help understanding leadership transitions or handover documentation. Another branch might have a question about organizational procedures. Sometimes a committee needs a route to the right national department; sometimes it needs someone to listen before the problem can be understood properly.

I wanted our team to be useful before an emergency happened. That meant visiting branch activities, introducing ourselves, building familiarity, and showing that the national organization could be a partner rather than a distant authority.

I couldn't do that by myself, and I didn't want to create a department that depended on one person being available for every decision.

Chapter 03

Building a Team of Eighteen

Our department had eighteen members in total, including me and my deputy. Sixteen team members were divided across four regional areas: North, East, West and South, with four people assigned to each region. People also took on responsibilities connected to documentation, finance, design and regional coordination.

When recruiting, I tried to look beyond whether someone seemed enthusiastic or confident in an interview. University commitments mattered. So did availability, resilience under pressure, existing relationships, and whether a person enjoyed planning ahead or was more comfortable taking action on the day.

I used scenarios to understand how candidates thought. What would they do if two colleagues were in conflict? How would they respond if another department requested help while their own team was overloaded? And how would they build a small team from people with different individual strengths?

There wasn't one ideal answer. I was interested in whether somebody could consider the people involved, their responsibilities, and the consequences of a decision rather than immediately reaching for a rule.

Of course, an interview isn't a perfect measure of a person. I had to be willing to discover that my first impression was incomplete and adapt responsibilities when necessary.

Chapter 04

The Environment I Wanted to Build

I wanted the department to be a place where volunteers could try things, make mistakes, and learn how to handle responsibilities independently.

That didn't mean pretending mistakes didn't matter. People had commitments, and other teams depended on their work. I expected someone who was falling behind to say so and help find a way to recover.

But I didn't want people to become afraid of admitting a problem. When something went wrong, I tried to speak privately with the person involved, understand the situation, and work out what could still be repaired. For decisions with serious consequences, I would take responsibility for making the call and explain why.

I also had to practice giving people autonomy when I could already imagine how I would solve something. Sometimes the more helpful thing was to let another person develop their own judgment while knowing I was there if they needed a second opinion.

Chapter 05

Leadership Development Camp — LDK 5.0

One of the most substantial programmes I chaired was LDK PPI Jerman 5.0, held in Herbstein from 1 to 3 May 2026.

The camp had its own organizing team of seventeen volunteers, including me, distributed across five divisions. That was a separate team from the eighteen people in Organizational Affairs.

I had been shaped by attending an earlier leadership camp. I wanted participants to leave with a sense that leadership wasn't reserved for flawless people. It could be something they learned by trying, working with others, and accepting responsibility.

Preparing the event meant balancing volunteers' studies with planning deadlines, delegating responsibility, helping division leads avoid doing everything themselves, and keeping enough room in the schedule for inevitable changes.

One significant problem arrived shortly before the event: a bus arrangement that had been confirmed months earlier was canceled. Because we had prepared backup vehicles and drivers, the team could reorganize the transfer into a shuttle arrangement. It wasn't our original plan, but it allowed us to respond without treating the entire event as a crisis.

The moment I remember most warmly came after the sessions were finished. The catering team was still cleaning the kitchen while other divisions had completed their jobs. I called people from those divisions to come and help. They did, and the work became something we finished together.

For me, that was leadership expressed in a very ordinary action: recognizing that a teammate still needed help, even after your own task was done.

Chapter 06

Giving Regional Teams Ownership

The regional meetings gave our department a different kind of opportunity. I wanted the local coordinators to plan and run their gatherings rather than wait for me to direct every detail.

I acted as an adviser and a person they could call when a decision was difficult. I also helped practically when something needed carrying, setting up or finishing. But the programmes belonged to the regional teams who prepared them.

In Hamburg, four northern coordinators organized a gathering at the Indonesian Consulate General. One of the discussion themes was Regenerasi: Beyond Zero — the question of what one committee can leave for the next.

The conversation explored how realistic plans, clear roles, timelines, documentation and opportunities for new leaders to learn could help an organization continue instead of starting from nothing after every leadership change.

I liked the idea behind it: regeneration isn't a reset. It's a continuation.

In Berlin, at the Indonesian Embassy, the regional team led a discussion involving Sportfest. In Frankfurt, the South and West teams came together, which created additional coordination challenges. We had many people willing to contribute, but aligning schedules and dividing responsibility became more complicated.

Those events were reminders that giving people ownership means being thoughtful about how much responsibility each person actually has, not simply involving as many people as possible.

Chapter 07

Branch contacts and One-on-one Meetings

Another important part of our work was meeting directly with local branch committees through one-on-one conversations.

We introduced the department, asked about their activities and difficulties, and tried to understand what support they needed. Sometimes the useful response was an explanation of a procedure. Sometimes it was introducing two branches facing similar challenges so they could learn from one another.

Chapter 08

Writing the branch procedures

We also worked on written procedures for branches that might need to pause activities, reactivate, split or merge. My deputy, a Deputy Secretary General and I developed draft guidance while checking it against PPI Jerman's governing rules, the AD/ART. Our aim was to make difficult transitions easier to understand. I wouldn't claim those drafts became binding nationwide without confirmation.

I wanted local branches to feel that asking for help was normal, not a sign that they'd failed to run their organization properly.

Chapter 09

What Leadership Taught Me

Before leading this team, I often thought that if I understood a problem clearly enough, I should be able to solve it. Working with volunteers taught me how incomplete that idea could be.

People have different capacities. A teammate may care deeply and still be overwhelmed by exams. Somebody may need guidance in one area and complete independence in another. A carefully reasoned decision can still fail if it ignores how the people involved will experience it.

I became more convinced that anger wasn't a useful foundation for leadership. Accountability matters, but so does the way we speak to someone who has made a mistake.

I also learned that I couldn't ask everyone else to rely on the team if I refused to rely on them myself.

I don't know how every person in our department will remember that period. I hope they remember being trusted to make decisions, encouraged to learn, and able to ask for help when a problem was bigger than they expected.

That's closer to the kind of leadership I want to keep practicing.
