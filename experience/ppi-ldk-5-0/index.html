Inside the leadership camp

LDK PPI Jerman 5.0

At LDK PPI Jerman 5.0 in Herbstein, I chaired a separate 17-person volunteer organising team. When a previously confirmed bus fell through shortly before the event, the team used backup cars for a shuttle. The moment I remember most was after the formal sessions, when people from other divisions came to help the exhausted kitchen team finish cleaning. It reminded me that leadership depends as much on trust and noticing people as it does on plans.

Chapter 01

What an Earlier Camp Gave Me

A few years before I helped organise LDK PPI Jerman 5.0, I had attended the second edition as a participant. I remember meeting people whose leadership positions had sounded impressive from a distance, then discovering that they were ordinary students who still had doubts and things to learn.

That experience changed how I saw leadership. I didn't have to wait until I became a flawless person before I could take responsibility. I could learn by taking it seriously, listening to others and being willing to make mistakes.

When I became organising chair for LDK 5.0, held in Herbstein from 1 to 3 May 2026, I wanted participants to have an opportunity to discover something similar for themselves. I didn't know whether any one person would leave feeling the way I had years earlier. But that was part of what motivated me throughout the preparation.

Chapter 02

Seventeen Volunteers, Five Divisions

The organising committee had seventeen volunteers in total, including me, working across five divisions. This was a separate team from the eighteen members in the PPI Jerman Organizational Affairs Department.

We were students with different schedules, strengths and amounts of time available. When forming the committee and allocating responsibilities, I paid attention to who preferred detailed preparation, who was comfortable with practical work on the event day, and how people might support one another.

I wanted division leads to have room to work independently. That also meant knowing when to ask for help. If somebody was falling behind, I encouraged them to explain what had changed and suggest a recovery plan rather than waiting until the last minute.

In logistics, I remember one division lead taking on too many tasks personally. We talked about delegation and ways to make the work easier for another person to pick up. Even a simple spreadsheet could help: a planned purchase was more useful when it included a source link, price and enough context that a teammate could follow it without starting the search again.

We used internal deadlines and some buffer time because I knew our original schedule would not survive every real-world dependency unchanged. When information from another department was late, we worked with provisional programme assumptions and updated the connected tasks when details arrived.

Chapter 03

When the Bus Disappeared

One of the clearest tests came shortly before the camp.

A city bus arrangement that had been confirmed roughly two months beforehand was canceled around two weeks before the event. We had participants arriving in different waves, and the original transport plan no longer worked.

Fortunately, the team had two cars and drivers available as a contingency. We worked out how to use them as a shuttle, planning around arrival times, loading and unloading, and repeated trips between the station and the venue. Each journey was short, but multiple arrivals meant the schedule still needed careful coordination.

It wasn't the transport solution we had expected to use. It was a reminder of why a backup matters only when you have thought about how it could actually be put into operation.

Chapter 04

The Moment After the Programme

The event brought together students from branches around Germany. According to Metro TV, 90 people attended across participants and organizers from 19 branches. That figure includes the committee; it does not mean ninety participants plus staff.

But one of my most memorable moments happened after the formal programme was over.

Most divisions had finished their work. The kitchen and catering team hadn't. They had spent the event preparing food and taking care of everyone, and they were still cleaning while the rest of us were ready to leave.

I called on people from other divisions to come and help.

They did, willingly. For a little while there was no division whose task was more important than another. We simply had teammates who still needed support.

That moment stayed with me because it was a practical expression of the kind of culture I'd hoped to build. Empathy wasn't a slogan in a planning document. It was noticing another person's workload and doing something about it.

Chapter 05

What I Carried Forward

Organising LDK was never a solo achievement. People prepared the programme, coordinated the venue and logistics, communicated with participants, cooked, cleaned, solved last-minute problems and supported one another in ways I couldn't have managed alone.

I learned that leading a volunteer team is partly about creating enough structure for people to work well, and partly about giving them enough trust to grow beyond the structure.

When I think back to the event, I remember the contingency plans and long preparations. But I also remember that kitchen, the volunteers coming back to help, and how much easier a difficult task became when it belonged to all of us.
