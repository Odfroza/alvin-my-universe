Indonesia at Kieler Woche

Six friends prepared an Indonesian food stand for Kieler Woche. Across its 2023 and 2024 editions, I helped with design, technical systems and on-site work while the team learned how to turn a first attempt into a better-run second year.

Chapter 01

Six Friends and One Idea

It started with a meeting between six Indonesian friends living in Kiel.

One of us, a doctor with an interest in business, had an idea: what if we opened an Indonesian food stand at Kieler Woche? He initiated and led the venture. The rest of us were invited to help make it possible.

Our first edition would be in June 2023, but the work began in 2022. We had to think about the size and layout of the stand, what food to sell, which equipment to rent or buy, where to get supplies, how to staff it, and which documents needed to go to the festival organisers before we could even secure a space.

Different people took responsibility for different parts of the preparation. I helped with interior design and decoration, the layout of the workspace, and technical questions such as electrical arrangements. I was also involved in cultural-performance coordination, helping find people who could appear on stage.

Through that work I established contact with the Indonesian Consulate General in Hamburg. The relationship also helped us borrow decorative materials, including Indonesian textiles, for the stand.

It was a lot of work for something visitors would experience during only a few days in June.

Chapter 02

Turning a Rented Booth Into a Working Stand

Once the applications and planning moved forward, we kept refining the details. People tested recipes, compared equipment and prices, considered staffing, and gathered materials. The people who would work at the stand needed schedules and an understanding of what each station would do.

About a week before the festival, the physical setup began. We rented the wooden booth structure, and the supplier assembled the shell. Our team was responsible for the inside.

We carried equipment out of storage, assembled tables and shelves, arranged workstations, positioned cooking and cleaning equipment, and worked on lighting and connections. We tested what we could and prepared the employees who would serve customers.

A floor plan can tell you whether furniture fits. It cannot fully tell you what happens when somebody carrying a hot dish needs to move past another person working at the fryer. That part of the design would be tested in public.

Chapter 03

The First Long Queues — 2023

The first edition, from 17 to 25 June 2023, was busier than we had expected.

The long queues were encouraging, but they also revealed weaknesses in our workflow. Card payments could be awkward. We didn't yet have a reliable system for recording every order and communicating it between the cashier and the people preparing food. Sometimes we depended too heavily on memory.

I came by the stand every day, checked whether the water, sinks, hot water and electricity were functioning, and helped when something broke or stopped working. Electrical breakers sometimes tripped, and we had to learn how to locate the problem and restore the affected service safely.

I also worked wherever I was needed: the cashier, frying area, grill, serving counter, or dishes. I wasn't exclusively a technician or a designer. During a busy shift, the important question was where another pair of hands would help most.

We learned the rhythm of the stand while running it. There were lunch and dinner rushes, times when the dishes accumulated faster than they could be cleaned, and moments when food sold out sooner than we'd expected.

As a team, we adjusted the workstations, clarified how to handle shortages, learned how to offer alternatives to customers, and began understanding when a shift needed more people. Many improvements were practical decisions made on the spot.

Chapter 04

A Taste of Indonesia

One of the happiest parts was seeing people discover Indonesian food.

We served a variety of warm, heavily seasoned meals, with both vegan and non-vegan choices. Vegetable fritters, or bala-bala, and seasoned tempeh offered visitors unfamiliar ways of enjoying vegan food. Pandan chiffon cake drew attention because of its bright green colour. Some people were skeptical until they tasted it.

For us, these were familiar flavours. For some visitors, they were a new experience.

I enjoyed explaining dishes to customers in German, especially because this was my first real experience in food service. I was still learning how to speak clearly while keeping a busy line moving. The conversations mattered as much as the food: for a moment, someone who had never tried a particular dish could discover something from home.

Chapter 05

Returning With Experience — 2024

When we prepared for the 2024 edition, we had something we couldn't have had the first time: a year of real operating experience.

We knew which hours tended to be busiest. We understood which stations needed more people, how food preparation could run behind, and why ordering and recordkeeping mattered. The stand was larger, and the team arranged a bigger preparation kitchen and a dishwasher.

Kitchen work also became more structured. In 2023 we'd underestimated how much extra cooking would still be needed after a full day at the stand, leaving volunteers preparing food late without a clear shift plan. In 2024, dedicated kitchen shifts made the work more predictable and reduced the pressure of having to do everything after closing.

We estimated stock requirements more carefully and planned shift assignments around busy periods. People could work more consistently at stations they'd already learned, rather than relearning an unfamiliar task whenever they arrived.

Our ordering system improved as well. We began using names and written notes to connect orders to customers. It was better than relying on memory, but it still wasn't ideal. We continued finding weaknesses in it during the second festival.

Chapter 06

Designing Something We Could Use Again

My own role developed in 2024. I took on design-management responsibility and worked with a small team rather than handling all of that preparation alone.

I helped design and assemble the interior, build tables and shelves, place equipment, and arrange lighting and practical infrastructure. Cultural-performance coordination was no longer part of my assignment that year.

The design question changed too. In 2023, we had mostly been trying to make the stand function for that edition. In 2024, we started considering which parts could be taken apart, stored and reused in future years.

That meant thinking not only about where something belonged, but also about how it would be assembled again, maintained, and moved.

Chapter 07

Better, Not Perfect — And What Stayed With Me

The second edition ran from 22 to 30 June 2024. Work felt more organized and the kitchen shifts were easier to manage, but there were still unexpected problems.

The dishwasher caused flooding at one point. Electrical interruptions still happened. The written-order system was an improvement, yet it could become confusing when the stand was busy.

We dealt with what happened and kept learning. That didn't turn us into a flawless operation. It meant we were less surprised by the kind of work that a busy food stand required.

From what I remember, our first year ended roughly around break-even because of initial purchases, while the second year made a profit. I don't have audited figures to present here. What I could see directly was that the team had built a much more manageable way of working.

One of my favourite memories from 2024 has nothing to do with a repair or an order.

Germany was hosting UEFA EURO 2024. I remember being at the stand when friends gathered around. We ate, talked and watched football while the festival continued around us.

It was an ordinary moment. Nobody was making a speech or celebrating a major milestone. We were just together.

But in the middle of a large German city festival, surrounded by visitors from many places, we had built somewhere that felt familiar. For a little while, it felt like a small piece of Indonesia in Kiel.

Kieler Woche was my first experience working in food service. I learned how to talk to customers, work across stations, respond to pressure, and understand what a schedule looks like when real demand doesn't follow it.

I also saw how quickly stress could affect a team's relationships. Under pressure, someone might get frustrated. Someone else might miss a problem because they were busy with their own task. The stand depended on people noticing when a teammate needed help, not simply completing their own assignment.

It was one of the first places where I began understanding empathy as something practical. It wasn't only about having good intentions. It was about what you did when another person was overwhelmed.

And I learned something about myself: when I feel ownership of a project, I want to be there for everything. That commitment can help a team, but it can also make it difficult for me to recognize my own limits.

We had begun with an idea and a rented wooden booth. Over two editions, we built, adjusted, made mistakes, and returned with better answers. None of it belonged to one person. What people experienced at the counter was the work of everyone behind it.
